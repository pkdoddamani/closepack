Keeping your voice: why commentary should stay bookkeeper-owned

By Praveen Doddamani · 2026-10-13

AI tools can now write a paragraph about any P&L. That is useful. It is also a reason to be careful about who owns the words your clients read every month.

Management accounts commentary is the few sentences that tell a client what their numbers mean. It is often the most-read part of a pack and the part that most clearly shows your expertise. As AI-generated commentary becomes a common feature in reporting software, it is worth thinking about what should be automated and what should not.

What good bookkeeper commentary does

Good commentary is not a restatement of the numbers. "Revenue was up compared to last month" is something the client can see on the chart. Useful commentary does at least one of these:

  • Explains why. "Revenue rose because the spring service backlog cleared in August."
  • Separates noise from signal. "Rent looks high this month because two months were invoiced together — nothing has changed."
  • Points to a decision. "Margins on parts have slipped for three months; worth reviewing supplier pricing."
  • Asks the right question. "Is the new hire in September still planned? It will show in wages next month."

Each of these depends on context that is not in the ledger: what you know about the business, what the owner told you last month, and what they care about.

What AI drafts are good at

To be fair to the tools, there are things automated drafts genuinely help with:

  • Spotting which lines moved most, so you do not miss anything.
  • Producing a consistent first draft structure, so you are not starting from a blank page.
  • Phrasing routine observations in plain English.

Used as a starting point, that can save real time and make your commentary more consistent across clients.

Where fully automated commentary goes wrong

It cannot know what it does not see

A draft only sees the export. It does not know that a large expense was a one-off you already discussed, or that a dip in sales was planned. Without that context it can confidently explain a movement the wrong way.

It sounds like everyone else

If many firms use similar tools, many clients will receive similar sentences. Generic commentary makes your reporting feel like a commodity — the opposite of what you want from the part of the pack that shows your judgement.

It blurs responsibility

Clients assume the commentary in your pack is your view. If it says something misleading, it is your name on it. Commentary you have not reviewed is a risk to the relationship, regardless of who drafted it.

It skips the conversation

Some of the most valuable commentary is a question rather than a statement. Deciding what to ask a client — and when — is a relationship judgement, not a text-generation task.

A workable model: draft, then own

The approach we think makes sense for most independent bookkeepers is simple: let software help with the draft, but treat every sentence as yours to keep, change or delete before it goes out. In practice:

  1. Let the tool flag movements. Biggest changes, unusual lines, trend breaks.
  2. Read the draft as a junior's notes. Useful observations, but not the final word.
  3. Add what only you know. Context from client conversations, plans, one-offs.
  4. Cut what does not matter. Three specific sentences beat ten generic ones.
  5. Write it the way you talk. If you would not say it on a call with this client, rewrite it.

This keeps the time saving where it helps — getting started — and keeps the judgement with you.

A simple structure for your management accounts narrative

If you want a consistent shape that still sounds like you, try three short parts:

  • What happened: one or two headline movements.
  • Why: your explanation, with context from the business.
  • What next: something to watch, decide or discuss.

It is short enough to write for every client and specific enough to be worth reading. It also pairs well with a split between assembly and debrief, covered in who builds the pack vs who owns the debrief.

The short version

AI can help you start your commentary faster. It should not decide what your clients hear. Keep the narrative bookkeeper-owned: use drafts for speed, add your context, and send only what you would say yourself.
