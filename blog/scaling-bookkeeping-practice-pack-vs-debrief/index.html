Who builds the pack vs who owns the client debrief?

By Praveen Doddamani · 2026-10-13

When you are the whole firm, you build every pack and have every client conversation. Somewhere around 30 to 50 monthly clients, that stops working. Here is a way to split the job without losing what clients value.

Most solo bookkeepers start out doing everything: closing, reporting, writing the commentary and talking it through with the client. That is fine at ten clients. As the list grows, monthly reporting becomes a bottleneck — not because any single pack is hard, but because the same steps repeat for everyone in the same few days of the month.

Two different jobs hiding in one task

"Doing the monthly pack" usually bundles two very different jobs:

Assembly

Exporting reports, dropping numbers into a template, updating charts and KPIs, checking formatting, producing the PDF. Process-driven, repeatable, and checkable against the ledger.

Narrative and debrief

Deciding what the numbers mean for this particular client, writing the commentary, and walking them through it. Judgement-driven, relationship-driven, and hard to hand off.

When these are treated as one task, the person with the client relationship spends a large part of their month on assembly. When they are split, each part can be done by the right person — or the right tool.

Why the split matters as you grow

Scaling a bookkeeping practice usually means one of three things: hiring, raising prices, or getting more efficient. The pack/debrief split helps with all three:

  • Hiring. Assembly is a well-defined task you can train a junior or a bookkeeping firm admin to do, with a clear checklist.
  • Pricing. The debrief is what clients experience as advice. Protecting time for it makes it easier to justify reporting as a valued service rather than an add-on.
  • Efficiency. Assembly is the part that benefits most from templates and tooling, because it is the same shape every month.

A simple way to divide the work

The assembler

This could be an admin, a junior bookkeeper, or you on a "production day". Their responsibilities:

  • Run exports using saved report settings.
  • Produce the draft pack in the standard template.
  • Tick a short checklist: totals tie to the ledger, comparison period is correct, client name and period are right.
  • Flag anything odd ("marketing costs look high compared to last month") without trying to explain it.

The owner of the debrief

Usually the bookkeeper who knows the client. Their responsibilities:

  • Review the flagged items and the draft.
  • Write or edit the commentary in their own voice.
  • Decide what to raise with the client and how.
  • Send the pack and hold the conversation.

The handover point is a draft pack that is numerically correct and visually consistent, with flags but no conclusions.

What goes wrong when the split is unclear

  • The assembler writes the story. Commentary drafted by someone who does not know the client tends to be generic, or occasionally wrong in ways that cost trust.
  • The owner re-does the assembly. If the template is not standard, the senior person ends up reformatting anyway, and no time is saved.
  • Nobody owns the deadline. Packs drift later into the month because each step waits on the other.

A short written process — even a one-page checklist — avoids most of this.

Where tools fit in

Assembly is the obvious place for software. A tool that takes a QuickBooks Online or Xero export and produces a consistent, branded draft can do much of what the assembler does. That does not remove the need for a human check, but it can shorten it.

The debrief is different. A tool can suggest a first draft of commentary, but the decision about what matters for this client — and the conversation itself — should stay with the person who owns the relationship. We explain why in keeping your voice in management accounts commentary.

A practical starting point

  1. Time one month of packs, separating assembly from commentary and debrief. (Our post on how long a monthly pack should take has a simple method.)
  2. Standardise one template for most clients.
  3. Write a one-page assembly checklist.
  4. Hand assembly for a few simple clients to someone else — or to a tool — for one month.
  5. Review what came back: what did you still have to fix? Update the checklist.

Done this way, growing from 20 to 40 clients does not have to mean doubling the hours you personally spend in spreadsheets during month-end.

The short version

Building the pack and owning the debrief are different jobs. Splitting them lets you delegate the repeatable part and keep the part clients actually pay attention to: your read on their business.
