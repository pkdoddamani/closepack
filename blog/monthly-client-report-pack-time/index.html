How long should a monthly client report pack take?

By Praveen Doddamani · 2026-10-13

There is no single right number. But there is a useful way to look at your bookkeeper reporting time — and most of the hours tend to hide in the parts clients never see.

If you send monthly management accounts packs to clients, you have probably asked yourself some version of this question at 9pm on the fourth working day of the month. The honest answer is "it depends" — on the client, the complexity, and what the pack is for. That answer is not very helpful on its own, so this post breaks the work into pieces you can actually measure and improve.

What a monthly management accounts pack actually involves

Before you can say how long a pack should take, it helps to separate the work into stages. For most independent bookkeepers it looks something like this:

  1. Close the books. Reconciliations, accruals, coding queries, chasing receipts. This is the real accounting work, and it has to be right.
  2. Export the numbers. Pull a P&L, balance sheet, and maybe a comparison period out of QuickBooks Online or Xero.
  3. Assemble the pack. Paste into a template, rebuild charts, fix formatting, recalculate month-on-month changes, add a logo, export to PDF.
  4. Write the commentary. Explain what moved and why, in language the client understands.
  5. Review and send. Check it, attach it, write the covering email.

Stages 1 and 4 are where your expertise lives. Stage 3 is where time tends to disappear without anyone noticing.

Where reporting time leaks

Ask a bookkeeper how long their packs take and they will usually quote a figure that blends all five stages together. That makes it hard to improve anything. When you separate them, a few common leaks show up:

  • Re-building the same chart every month. A spreadsheet chart that needs its ranges nudged each time is a small task repeated across every client.
  • Formatting drift. Fonts, column widths and page breaks that shift when the export layout changes slightly.
  • Copy-paste checks. Re-verifying that the numbers you pasted match the numbers in the ledger, because the paste step is where errors creep in.
  • Per-client snowflakes. A slightly different template for each client, so nothing can be reused cleanly.
  • Starting commentary from a blank page. Writing "revenue increased compared to last month" from scratch, before you get to the part that actually matters.

None of these require judgement. All of them take time, and they multiply with your client count.

A better question: how long does each stage take?

Instead of asking "how long should a pack take?", try timing one month of packs by stage. You do not need a time-tracking system — a note next to each client is enough. Record roughly how long you spent on:

  • closing the books,
  • assembling and formatting,
  • writing commentary, and
  • review and send.

Once you see the split for your own practice, you can decide what to protect and what to cut. Closing time is usually not worth squeezing — that is the quality of your work. Commentary time is often worth more, not less, because it is what the client values. Assembly time is the obvious candidate for standardising.

What a sensible target looks like

Rather than chasing a specific number of minutes, aim for these properties:

  • Assembly is boring and repeatable. Same structure every month, same structure across clients, with only the data changing.
  • Your effort scales with complexity, not with formatting. A simple client should take noticeably less time than a complex one. If they take about the same, formatting is probably dominating.
  • Commentary gets your best attention. You arrive at the writing step with energy left, rather than after an hour of fighting a spreadsheet.
  • Review is quick because there is less to check. Fewer manual pastes means fewer places for numbers to go wrong.

How to cut assembly time without cutting quality

Standardise one template

Pick one pack structure that works for most of your clients: a short KPI snapshot, a trend view, commentary, and the detailed statements. Resist the urge to customise per client unless there is a real reason. If a client needs something extra, add it as an appendix rather than reshaping the whole pack.

Fix your export settings

Save report settings in QuickBooks Online or Xero so the export layout is identical each month. Consistent inputs are what make any template — spreadsheet or tool — reliable.

Keep commentary in a consistent shape

A simple structure helps: what happened, why it happened, and what to watch or discuss. That keeps you focused on the substance and makes packs easier for clients to read month after month. We go deeper on this in why commentary should stay bookkeeper-owned.

Consider whether assembly needs to be you

As your client list grows, assembly is often the first thing you can hand to someone else — or to a tool — while you keep the client conversation. See who builds the pack vs who owns the debrief.

What clients notice (and what they do not)

Clients rarely notice how long a pack took. They notice whether it arrived on time, whether they could understand it, and whether it told them something useful. A clean, consistent pack with a few clear sentences about what changed tends to land better than a long document they skim. There is more on this in default QBO/Xero PDFs vs branded client packs.

The short answer

A monthly client report pack should take as long as the closing and the thinking require — and as little as possible beyond that. If you measure your time by stage, you will probably find that formatting and assembly are taking a bigger share than you would choose. That is the part worth fixing first.
