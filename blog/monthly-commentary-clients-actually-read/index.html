How to write monthly commentary clients actually read

By Praveen Doddamani · 2026-11-09

Most monthly packs are opened for the commentary, not the full P&L. If the first few sentences are vague or padded, the rest of the report rarely gets a fair look. This post is about writing commentary clients finish — without turning yourself into a full-time writer.

We have already argued that commentary should stay bookkeeper-owned rather than handed off to an AI replacement. That still leaves a craft question: what does a useful monthly note look like on the page, month after month, when you have limited time and several clients waiting?

Lead with the one thing that matters this month

Open with a single sentence about the month's main story. Not a summary of every line, and not a polite throat-clearing paragraph. Something like: "Cash was tight this month even though profit looked fine, mostly because stock purchases landed before the big invoice went out." Or: "Revenue was quiet, but margins held and there is nothing here that needs a decision this week."

That first sentence does two jobs. It tells the owner whether they need to keep reading carefully, and it shows that someone who knows the business has already looked. If you bury the real point three paragraphs down, many clients will never reach it.

Keep it short enough to finish on a phone

A useful default for many small-business packs is roughly half a page to one page of commentary: three to six short paragraphs, or a few bullet points with a short wrap-up. Long enough to cover the month; short enough that it does not feel like homework.

If a point needs more space, put the detail in a later section of the pack and point to it from the commentary. "Supplier costs on parts are up again — see the budget vs actual page for the year-to-date view" is clearer than repeating the whole table in prose. Our notes on budget vs actual reports for small business clients cover how that page can carry the weight.

Explain movement, do not restate the chart

Clients can already see that revenue rose or wages fell. Restating the obvious wastes the most valuable inches of the pack. Useful commentary answers the next question: why did it move, is it temporary, and does anything need a decision?

  • Why. A backlog cleared, a job slipped into next month, a one-off refund, a new hire started mid-month.
  • Noise vs signal. Two months of rent billed together is noise. Three months of slipping margin is signal.
  • Decision or question. "Worth reviewing that supplier," or "Is the October hire still planned?"

If you cannot say why something moved, say that honestly and ask. "I am not sure what drove the spike in contractor costs — can you confirm whether Project X finished early?" is more useful than a vague "contractor costs were higher."

Use a stable shape so writing gets faster

Blank-page commentary is what makes the month feel heavy. A simple repeating structure reduces that friction without making every client sound the same:

  1. One-line headline for the month.
  2. Profit and cash in plain English (especially when they disagree).
  3. Two or three line items that actually moved, with cause.
  4. Anything the client needs to do or decide.
  5. A short look ahead if you already know something is coming.

The shape stays the same; the content changes with the business. That is also where a draft from your reporting tools can help: as a skeleton to edit, not as the finished voice. We go into that distinction in why commentary should stay bookkeeper-owned.

Write for the owner, not for another accountant

Swap jargon for the words the client already uses. "Gross margin" may be fine for some owners and meaningless for others. "What you keep after the cost of the bikes" might be clearer for a retail client. Match the vocabulary you already use in calls and emails.

Avoid hedging that adds length without adding meaning: "it would appear that," "broadly speaking," "as can be seen from the attached." Prefer short sentences. Prefer concrete nouns. Prefer one clear claim per paragraph.

Make the next action obvious

Commentary that only describes the past is easy to skim and forget. Where it helps, end with one clear next step for the client or for you:

  • "Please confirm the three open card transactions by Thursday so I can close cleanly."
  • "If you want, we can set a simple monthly sales target so next month's pack has something to compare against."
  • "No action needed this month — I will watch cash again after the November invoices go out."

"No action needed" is a valid ending. Owners like knowing when they can put the report down.

Protect quality when the month is busy

On heavy months, cut breadth before you cut clarity. Better to explain three real movements well than to mention twelve lines with no cause. Skip the filler paragraph that says everything was "broadly in line with expectations" unless that truly is the story — and even then, say why that is good news for this particular client.

If assembly work is eating the time you need for commentary, that is a process problem, not a writing problem. Standardising how the pack is built is usually a better fix than writing shorter forever. The pack should make your commentary easy to find, not compete with it for attention.

A quick self-check before you send

  • Can the owner understand the first sentence without opening any other page?
  • Did I explain at least one "why," not only a "what"?
  • Is there a clear decision, question, or "no action needed"?
  • Would I be happy reading this aloud on a call with the client?

If those four land, the commentary is doing its job. The numbers still matter. The branding still helps. But the few sentences at the front are what turn a monthly PDF into advice the client remembers.
