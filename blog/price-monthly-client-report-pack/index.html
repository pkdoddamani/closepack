How to price and package a monthly client report as a premium service

By Praveen Doddamani · 2026-11-09

Many bookkeepers already build a monthly pack. Fewer price it as its own clear service. The result is that the most visible part of the relationship gets buried inside a fixed fee, and the work expands without the fee noticing. This post is about packaging the monthly report so clients understand what they are buying — and so you can charge for it with a straight face.

Nothing here assumes a huge firm or a polished product suite. It is aimed at independent bookkeepers who already close the books and want the reporting side to feel intentional rather than bolted on.

Separate the close from the pack in your own mind

Before you can price a pack, you need to know what it is. For most practices the monthly cycle has two different jobs:

  • Closing the books. Reconciliations, coding, chasing missing items, getting the ledger right. This is the foundation.
  • Reporting the month. Turning those numbers into something the owner can read: headline KPIs, trends, a short commentary, and a clean PDF that looks like it came from you.

Clients often experience both as "my bookkeeper sends the month." You do not have to sell them as two invoices. You do need to know which hours belong where, because otherwise every request to "just add a cash chart" lands on unpaid time. Our earlier note on how long a monthly client report pack should take is a useful way to split those stages before you set a price.

Decide what "the pack" includes

A vague deliverable is hard to price and easy to expand. Write a one-page definition you can reuse in proposals and onboarding. A practical default for many small clients:

  • A branded cover or header with your practice name and the client's.
  • A one-page summary: profit, cash, and two or three KPIs that matter for that business.
  • P&L and balance sheet for the month, with a comparison period.
  • Short written commentary you edit and own.
  • A covering email that points to the headline and any open questions.

Optional add-ons can sit outside the base pack: budget vs actual, aged receivables detail, project or job profitability, a quarterly deeper review. Naming the base clearly makes it easier to say yes to extras without giving them away.

If you are still sending a default software PDF with no narrative, the first packaging step may simply be moving to a branded pack clients can actually read. We compare those two approaches in default QBO/Xero PDF vs branded client pack.

Price from time and value, not from guilt

There is no universal right fee. A workable way to set one:

  1. Time a few real packs end to end, split into close, assembly and commentary.
  2. Decide a target effective hourly rate for your reporting work.
  3. Check that number against what the pack replaces for the client: a messy DIY spreadsheet, a delayed decision, or a vague sense of "I never know how we did."

If assembly currently takes longer than commentary, fix the process before you inflate the price to cover messy templates. Clients pay for insight and reliability. They should not be funding formatting debt forever.

Some bookkeepers include a basic pack in the monthly bookkeeping fee and charge for a fuller version. Others put every client on the same pack and raise the monthly fee once. Either can work. What tends not to work is promising "management accounts" in the proposal and delivering an unannotated export because the month got busy.

How to talk about it with existing clients

If you have been sending packs informally, a sudden price conversation can feel awkward. A calm frame helps:

  • Show one recent pack and name what is already included.
  • Explain the rhythm: when it arrives, what it covers, what you need from them to keep it on time.
  • Offer a clear upgrade if you are adding substance (commentary, KPIs, branding) rather than only renaming the same PDF.
  • Give a date when the new packaging starts, with enough notice to feel fair.

You do not need a dramatic sales pitch. Most owners already want someone to tell them what the month meant. They are more likely to push back on vagueness than on a defined, useful deliverable.

Make the pack look like a product

Packaging is not only a price list. Consistency is part of the product:

  • Same section order every month.
  • Same send window each month, as far as client delays allow.
  • Same file naming and branding.
  • Commentary that sounds like you, not like a template dumped into the PDF.

When the pack arrives looking finished, clients treat it as a finished service. When it arrives looking improvised, they treat every request as a small free favour.

Protect scope without sounding defensive

Scope creep usually arrives as friendly extras: "Can you also include a forecast?" "Can we get this by Tuesday for the bank?" Answer with options, not with silent overtime.

  • "Happy to add a simple forecast as a quarterly add-on."
  • "I can prioritise this pack for the bank meeting; it will land on Thursday instead of the usual Friday slot for your other entity."
  • "That sits outside the monthly pack — here is what it would involve."

Clear packaging makes those replies easier because you already defined the base.

Where tools fit, without overselling them

Software can help with assembly: pulling exports into a consistent branded PDF so you spend more time on commentary and less on layout. That is useful. It is not a substitute for knowing the client, and it should not be sold as if the tool writes the advice.

ClosePack is being built for that assembly step in a founding pilot: QuickBooks Online or Xero export in, branded pack with KPIs, trends and draft commentary out — then you edit and own the words. Whether you use a tool or a careful template, the commercial point is the same. Package the reporting work deliberately, price it as something real, and keep the judgement with the bookkeeper.

A simple packaging checklist

  • Written list of what the monthly pack includes and what it does not.
  • Target send window and what happens if client inputs are late.
  • Fee or fee component that covers reporting, not only transaction processing.
  • One sample pack you are proud to show a prospect.
  • A short paragraph in your proposal that describes the pack in plain English.

Do those five and the monthly report stops being "something I also do" and becomes a service you can stand behind. That is good for cash flow. It is also good for clients, who finally know what they are meant to open each month.
