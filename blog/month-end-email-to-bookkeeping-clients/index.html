How to write the month-end email that goes with your client's report

By Praveen Doddamani · 2026-10-26

Most bookkeepers put real care into the monthly report and then send it with a one-line email: "Please find attached your reports for September." That email is often the only part of the month's work the client is sure to read. It is worth a few more minutes.

This post covers what a good month-end email to a bookkeeping client does, a simple structure you can reuse, how to handle open questions and bad news, and an editable template at the end.

Why the covering email matters more than it looks

Busy owners triage their inbox. A PDF attachment is a task; a short email body is something they can read on their phone between jobs. If the body tells them the two or three things that matter this month, many will act on those even if they never open the attachment. If the body says nothing, the report may sit unread until tax time.

The email is also where your client relationship shows. It is the difference between "the books are done" and "here is what happened in your business, and here is what I need from you". The second one is what clients remember when they decide whether your fee is worth it.

What a good month-end email does

A useful covering email usually does four jobs, in this order:

  1. Confirms the month is closed. One sentence saying which period is done and that the report is attached or linked.
  2. Gives the headline. Two or three plain-language points: the result for the month, the cash position, and anything unusual.
  3. Lists what you need from them. Missing receipts, uncategorised transactions, questions about a payment. Numbered, specific, with a date if it matters.
  4. Points to what is next. An upcoming tax deadline, a payroll change, or simply when the next report will arrive.

That is it. Anything more detailed belongs in the report itself, where it has room for comparisons and context.

Keep the headline short and in plain language

The headline should read like something you would say out loud on a call. Instead of "Gross margin decreased 4 percentage points month on month", try "Margin dipped this month, mostly because materials costs went up on the Smith job." Name the cause if you know it. If you do not, say so and ask.

Two or three points is the right number. If you list seven, nothing stands out. A good habit is to pick the one thing you would want the owner to remember if they read nothing else, and put it first.

Be careful not to drift into advice you are not engaged to give. Pointing out that receivables are growing is part of reporting; telling the client to change their pricing may not be. Keep the observation factual and offer a conversation if it needs one.

The headline should also match the commentary in the report. If the email says "a solid month" and the report shows a loss, the client will trust neither. We wrote more about why that narrative should stay in your own words in keeping your voice in management accounts commentary.

How to ask clients for missing information

Questions buried in a paragraph get skipped. A few habits make them much more likely to get answered:

  • Number them. The client can reply "1. Yes, 2. That was the van repair" without rewriting anything.
  • Give the detail they need to answer. Date, amount and payee, not "a few transactions need checking".
  • Say what happens if they do not answer. For example, "If I don't hear back, I'll leave this in suspense and it will show as uncategorised." That is not a threat; it just explains the effect on their numbers.
  • Keep the list short. If there are twenty open items, send the top three in the email and the rest in an attached list or a shared sheet.

Many of these questions are easier to collect if you work through them as part of your close rather than at the end. Our month-end close checklist for bookkeepers starts with getting client inputs in and clearing uncategorised items before the report goes out.

Delivering bad news without alarming the client

Some months the numbers are not good: a loss, a cash squeeze, a large unexpected bill. The email should not hide this, and it should not be the first time the client hears about something serious. A few principles help:

  • State it plainly and early. Owners usually prefer a straight sentence to a softened one they have to decode.
  • Add the context you know. A loss in a month with an annual insurance payment is different from a loss with no obvious cause.
  • Offer a call for anything significant. If cash may not cover next month's payroll, a short call is better than a paragraph.

Practical details that save back-and-forth

  • Use a consistent subject line, such as "September 2026 financial report – [Business name]". It makes the report easy to find later, for you and the client.
  • Send around the same time each month. Clients start to expect it, and a predictable schedule makes it easier to chase your own missing information.
  • Attach one file, not five. A single combined pack is easier to open on a phone than separate exports for each report.
  • Send to the right person. Sometimes the person who sends you receipts is not the person who makes decisions. Ask who should receive the report.

An editable month-end email template

Here is a simple template you can adapt. Replace the bracketed parts and cut anything that does not apply that month.

Subject: [Month Year] financial report – [Business name]

Hi [Name],

[Month] is closed and your report is attached.

The short version:

• [Result for the month in one sentence, with the main reason.]
• [Cash position and whether it went up or down.]
• [Anything unusual: a one-off cost, a large invoice still unpaid, a seasonal dip.]

A few things I need from you, ideally by [date]:

1. [Transaction: date, amount, payee] – what was this for?
2. [Missing receipt or document.]
3. [Any other question.]

Coming up: [deadline, payroll change, or "Your next report will arrive around (date)."]

Happy to talk through any of this on a quick call.

[Your name]

The template is a starting point, not a script. Your best emails will sound like you, and clients will notice when they do.
