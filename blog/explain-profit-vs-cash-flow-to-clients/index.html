How to explain profit vs cash flow to your bookkeeping clients

By Praveen Doddamani · 2026-10-19

Sooner or later, almost every small business client asks some version of the same question: "The report says we made a profit, so why is there nothing in the bank?" How you answer it shapes whether they trust the rest of your monthly report.

The question is fair. Owners see the bank balance every day, and the profit and loss report only once a month. When the two tell different stories, the bank usually wins, and the report starts to look like accounting theory rather than something useful. The fix is not a lecture on accrual accounting. It is a short, repeatable way of showing the client where the gap came from, using their own numbers.

Why profit and cash drift apart

Before explaining it to a client, it helps to have your own short list of the usual causes. For most small businesses, the gap between profit and the change in the bank balance comes from a handful of things:

  • Customers who have not paid yet. Sales invoiced this month count toward profit, but the cash arrives when the customer pays. Growing receivables quietly absorb cash.
  • Bills not yet paid. The reverse effect: costs recorded this month but paid next month make cash look better than profit for a while.
  • Stock bought ahead of sales. Buying inventory uses cash now, but it only becomes a cost in the P&L when it is sold.
  • Loan repayments and owner drawings. Paying down the principal on a loan, or taking money out as an owner, reduces cash without touching profit.
  • Equipment and other larger purchases. If they are capitalised, the cash goes out in one month while the cost reaches the P&L gradually through depreciation.
  • Tax timing. Sales tax or VAT collected sits in the bank until it is paid over, which can make a good month feel richer than it is.

In any given month, one or two of these usually explain most of the difference. Your job in the report is to find those one or two and name them, not to list everything that could theoretically matter.

Start from the bank balance, not the P&L

Clients think in cash, so start there. A simple walk from last month's bank balance to this month's tends to land better than a formal cash flow statement:

  1. Bank balance at the start of the month.
  2. Plus the profit for the month.
  3. Adjusted for the two or three items that moved the most: customers owing more or less, bills paid down or built up, stock, loan repayments, drawings.
  4. Equals the bank balance at the end of the month.

Kept to four or five lines, this bridge does most of the explaining for you. The client can see that the profit was real and that the cash went somewhere specific. It also tends to start the right conversation, such as chasing a slow-paying customer or rethinking how much stock to carry, rather than a conversation about whether the books are wrong.

QuickBooks Online and Xero both offer cash flow style reports you can use as a starting point. The formal statement is useful for you, but for most owner-managed businesses a short bridge in plain words is easier to read than the full layout.

Use the client's own words

Terms like "working capital" or "accruals" are accurate, but they make many owners stop reading. Try phrasing the same idea in the language they already use:

  • Instead of "receivables increased", try "customers owe you more at the end of the month than at the start, so some of this month's profit is still sitting with them."
  • Instead of "principal repayments", try "the loan payments come out of the bank, but only the interest shows up as a cost on the P&L."
  • Instead of "inventory build", try "you bought stock you have not sold yet, so that cash is on the shelf rather than in the bank."

Once you have phrasing that works for a client, reuse it. Consistent wording month to month makes the report easier to follow, and the client starts to recognise the pattern without needing it explained again.

Put it in the commentary, not just the conversation

Many bookkeepers explain the profit and cash gap well on a call, then send a report that only shows the P&L. The client forgets the call and asks again next month. Writing a short cash note into the monthly commentary fixes that. Two or three sentences are usually enough: what profit was, what happened to cash, and the one thing driving the difference.

This is also where your judgement matters most. Software can calculate the movements, but deciding which one is worth mentioning, and whether it is a concern or just timing, depends on knowing the client. We wrote more about this in why commentary should stay bookkeeper-owned.

Make it a fixed part of the monthly pack

If the profit vs cash question comes up often, give it a permanent place in the report rather than answering it ad hoc. A layout that works for many small clients:

  • A headline page with profit, cash at month end and one or two KPIs that matter for that business.
  • The short bank bridge described above.
  • Aged receivables, if customers paying late is a recurring theme.
  • The P&L and balance sheet for those who want the detail.
  • Your commentary, including a line on cash.

Putting the cash view early in the pack, before the detailed statements, answers the question most owners actually have before they get to the pages they find hardest to read. Check the numbers before you explain them

One caution: a gap between profit and cash is sometimes a sign that something in the books needs fixing, not explaining. Unmatched payments, duplicated bills or an unreconciled card account can all create a gap that looks like timing but is really an error. Make sure the close is done, with accounts reconciled and the period reviewed, before you write the cash note. That way, when you tell a client where their money went, you can be confident it is true.

The aim is simple: a client who reads your report should never have to wonder why the bank balance does not match the profit. A few clear lines each month are usually all it takes.
